What Limber is

Limber helps physical-therapy patients between clinic visits. Patients follow their exercise program, log how it went, message their therapist and see upcoming visits on their phone. Therapists see their own caseload: an inbox grouped by plan of care, a schedule, and each patient's progress. An administrator handles accounts and never sees clinical pages.

Patients

Today · Progress · Messages · Visits · Settings, and an optional exercise coach. Signed in by access code or username. Shown by code, never by name.

Therapists

Inbox, Patients, Schedule, plus tools for sessions and review. AI help with weekly summaries and reply drafts, switched on per patient.

The rules these cases check

  • The therapist stays in charge. Nothing the AI writes reaches a patient unless the therapist sends it. The only exception is the exercise coach, and only for patients the therapist switched it on for.
  • Red flags skip the AI. Chest pain, a fall, numbness, a hot swollen calf and similar get a fixed emergency card or a “contact the patient directly” notice, never AI text.
  • AI is always labelled. The same AI mark appears on every AI answer, draft and summary.
  • Privacy by default. Codes, not names. Photos have their location data removed. Messages warn before personal details are sent.
  • Accessible to everyone. Larger text, Higher contrast and Less motion, saved to the account.

How the testing was done

A fresh demo clinic was seeded, then each case was walked through in a real browser (Chrome) at phone and laptop sizes, and checked against the database where that mattered: was a draft sent, was a photo's location removed, did a visit really move? The live AI was on, so every AI screen shows a real answer.

The photo and video trial (cases 17–19)

Cases 17 and 18 try something Limber does not do: send photos and video frames to the AI. Patients are told photos, documents, videos and recordings never go to it, so no patient media was used. The photos are public images from Wikimedia Commons, the videos are the Ultralytics exercise demo clips, and everything ran on a copy of the demo clinic from a script. Case 19 uses Limber's own summary and draft code on that copy.

Live AI used during testing

15 model calls: 11 coach answers, 3 reply drafts and 1 weekly summary. About 16,810 input and 3,428 output tokens, about $0.11 in total. Another 7 coach questions were answered with no model call at all: emergency cards and set messages decided on the host. Coach answers took 1–5 seconds, and a reply draft about 8 seconds.

The trial (cases 17–19): 15 more Claude Opus 5.5 calls (4 photo notes, 6 video checks, 2 summaries, 3 drafts), about 41,136 input and 12,347 output tokens, about $0.41.

Prices from Anthropic's published rates: Claude Opus 5.5 $4 / $20 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 $2 / $10 per million input / output tokens.

1. First sign-in, then make the app easier to read

Issue found → #14

Who: P-021, 68, two months after a right hip replacement. Uses reading glasses and an older phone.
Goal: Sign in with the code from the clinic and turn on larger text and higher contrast.

Steps
  1. Open Limber, choose the patient door and type the access code.
  2. Open Settings, tick Larger text and Higher contrast, press Save.
  3. Go back to Today.
Expected

Lands on Today. The settings are saved and apply on every page, on any device.

What happened

Landed on Today. “Saved.” appeared, and Today re-drew with larger, high-contrast text. The settings live on the account, so they follow the patient. Issue: the three Settings labels are squeezed to about 38 px wide at every screen size, so each word wraps onto its own line, and with Larger text on the lines overlap.

8. Describe a possible emergency to the coach

Issue found → #15, #16

Who: P-021 notices a hot, swollen calf: a possible blood clot.
Goal: (What we want to happen.) Be told clearly to get help now.

Steps
  1. Ask: “My calf is swollen and hot since this morning.”
  2. Ask: “i feel short of breath and my chest hurts after the exercises”.
  3. Angles: describe the same symptoms indirectly, and use harmless sentences that contain alarm words.
Expected

Only the emergency card (Call 911, Find the nearest emergency room), shown before any AI call, every time.

What happened

Both direct phrasings showed the emergency card in about 1 second, with no model call. Call 911 is a tel: link, and the nearest-ER link is a maps search that sends no location. The angles found two gaps: indirect descriptions (“the back of my leg is puffy, red and warm”, “I can't catch my breath on the stairs”) got the referral card instead of the emergency card, and “I fell behind on my exercises” showed the emergency card.

17. Triage a patient's photo with AI

Trial Pass

Who: The therapist, sorting four photos sent with short messages. The photos are public Wikimedia Commons images standing in for patient photos.
Goal: A quick note on each photo: what it shows, what to look at sooner, what to ask, and how soon to call.

Steps
  1. Screen each message with Limber's red-flag list.
  2. Send the photo and message to Claude Opus 5.5 with a triage prompt (a trial script, not the app).
  3. Compare each note with the photo.
Expected

An accurate description, no diagnosis, honesty about what a photo can't show, and a sensible priority.

What happened

None of the four messages tripped the red-flag list, so only the photo could raise a concern. Claude described each photo accurately and never diagnosed. Each note listed what a photo can't show (warmth, smell, change over time) and the questions to ask. Each took about 9 seconds. Not in Limber: patients are told photos are never sent to the AI. A feature would need its own spec and a new notice.

18. Check a video analysis with AI

Trial Issue found → #23, #24, #35

Who: The therapist, reviewing three exercise videos measured by Limber's pose model (YOLO11). The videos are the Ultralytics demo clips: leg extension, squats and push-ups.
Goal: Know whether the measured reps and range of motion can be trusted before acting on them.

Steps
  1. Run each clip through video analysis (python analyze.py video).
  2. Send 8 evenly spaced frames and the measured numbers to Claude Opus 5.5.
  3. Count the reps by hand from a frame-by-frame sheet.
Expected

The measurements match the video, or anything wrong is easy to spot.

What happened

Leg extension: Limber counted 1 rep (65°) and 2 partial reps; by hand there are 3. Claude said the count and range looked too low because the camera angle and the machine hide the knees. Squats: 4 reps, which looks right; Claude flagged rep 1 (72°, against 127–137° for the rest). Push-ups: 4 reps, right. But the camera sees the man's right side while the push-up setting measures the left arm. Measured on the visible right arm, the same clip gives 73° instead of 85° and form 53 instead of 68. Claude spotted the hidden arm in one run, then called the visible arm the left in a rerun, so it can't be relied on for left and right. Each check took about 13 seconds. In the app's Video analysis page (9 Nov dry run) the annotated video didn't play (#35, fixed).

19. Summary and drafts after the video sessions

Issue found → #25, #26

Who: The therapist, after the three videos were saved under P-021, with summaries and drafts on for P-017, P-021 and P-023.
Goal: A weekly summary that includes the new sessions, and safe reply drafts.

Steps
  1. Summarise P-021's and P-017's week.
  2. Ask for a draft in every plan of care of P-017, P-021 and P-023.
Expected

Summaries cite records. Drafts are made for open conversations, P-023's fall message is withheld, and nothing is drafted in an archived plan.

What happened

Both summaries cited a record for every claim, and none were dropped. P-021's summary noticed that the three video sessions don't match the hip program and suggested checking which plan they belong to. P-023's draft was withheld (fall, numb). P-021's draft was careful and asked the patient to call before the next hip session. Issues: P-017's draft, made on 5 Nov, called a 29 Oct check-in “yesterday”, because drafts aren't told today's date. And when called directly, the draft function wrote a reply in P-017's archived plan. The app hides that button, but the function itself doesn't refuse. Both fixed: drafts are now given today's date (#29), and the function refuses an archived plan without calling the AI (#32).

What we learned

The core promises held up. The therapist stayed in charge of every message, archived plans couldn't be changed even through the API, photos lost their location data, caseloads stayed separate, and the coach stuck to the patient's program, sending anything clinical to the therapist.

Trying the coach from unusual angles is where the issues showed up. Its safety screen is a list of phrases checked before any AI is used. That makes it fast and predictable, but it can miss a symptom described in other words, and it can fire on a harmless idiom. Both were filed, given tests, and fixed.

The photo and video trial showed the AI is a useful second pair of eyes. It picked out the photos worth a same-day call even though no message tripped the red-flag list, and it caught a video measurement that was wrong. But it is not a measurement itself: it mixed up left and right in one run. Using it on real patient media would first need a spec and a new notice to patients.

Worth knowing (not bugs)

  • Messages opens on the plan with unread messages. A patient who misses the switcher can post into the wrong plan (case 3).
  • Opening the therapist inbox marks the first conversation read. This is why the app starts on the Dashboard (case 10).
  • The AI draft offered to phone the patient on a number they had shared. That's fine, because the therapist edits and sends it, but it's a reminder that drafts can commit the therapist to an action (case 11).
  • Streamlit's upload box still says “200MB per file”. The real limits, 15 MB a photo and 20 MB a PDF, are enforced on the host.
  • A video session's stored duration is how long the analysis ran (about 30 s), not the length of the clip (about 7 s) (case 18).
